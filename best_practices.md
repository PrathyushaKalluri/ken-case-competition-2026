## 1. What actually wins The Ken's Case Competition — evidence from 3 years

### 1.1 The judging criteria have evolved, and the change tells you what to prioritize

| Year | Criteria | What's new/different |
|---|---|---|
| 2024 (inaugural) | Innovation, Clarity, Feasibility, Thoroughness, **Context** (unique to the company/competitors/situation) | "Context" — no real primary research requirement; company/market analysis was enough |
| 2025 | Evidence, Creativity, Clarity, Feasibility, Thoroughness | **"Context" replaced by "Evidence"** — the single biggest formal shift: judges now explicitly require proof a real person told you something, not just sharp company analysis |
| 2026 | Evidence, Creativity, Clarity, Feasibility, Thoroughness (same 5, explicitly: *"AI-generated solutions usually fail"* on Creativity) | Same 5 as 2025, but now paired with a 10-question, word-capped form (not free-form decks) and an explicit warning about generic AI-flavored answers |

**Read this trend correctly**: The Ken has been steadily raising the bar on *primary evidence* and lowering tolerance for polished-but-generic strategy. A beautifully argued business case with no real customer behind it would have been competitive in 2024. It will not be competitive in 2026 — Q2 (the one customer insight) is explicitly the answer judges "read first."

### 1.2 What judges have said, in their own words, about what fails

- **Deepak Shenoy (2024 judge, CEO Capitalmind)**, on financial claims: *"I'll ask about where's the money. I'll care about profits, and will we get that without using the words 'adjusted' and 'ebitda'."* — **Lesson: never hide behind financial jargon. State the real number, or don't claim it.**
- **The Ken's own team (2025 semifinalist commentary)**, on tech claims: *"The sentences 'we will leverage AI/ML' and 'we will do personalisation' were liberally thrown around all over decks and answers"* — flagged as superficial. The one team (ROI Rangers) that "truly spent the time and effort to create and visualise a tech-first solution ground-up" was called out specifically as the exception that made the finals. — **Lesson: never name a technology without naming the specific mechanism it enables. "AI-powered" is not a claim; "an AI that cross-references warranty status against a 90-second symptom description before assigning a technician" is.**
- **2026's own page, verbatim**: *"This is usually where AI-generated solutions fail"* (on Creativity). — **Lesson, stated by the organiser itself: the judges can tell when a solution was pattern-matched by an LLM instead of built from a real, specific, non-obvious observation. The insight must be something that could only come from talking to a real person.**

### 1.3 What made finalists actually win, across the 30+ teams reviewed

Patterns that repeat across winners and finalists in both the 2024 quick-commerce format and 2025 incumbent-disruption format (from `files/ken_case_competition_2025_winning_submissions.txt`, the 2024 shortlist writeup, and the winner announcement):

1. **Audacity, backed by rigor — never audacity alone.** Tek-Ken's "five-minute delivery, 10 minutes feels old" pitch was the boldest claim in 2024 — and it won a finals slot *because* it came with detailed financial models, primary research, and a new store-format concept ("Lite store"), not because it was bold. Metamorphosis (the eventual 2024 winner) explicitly wrote in their own pitch: *"we have come up with some truly bold ideas, but don't mistake bold with reckless (ours are logically foolproof)."* **The pattern is bold claim + demonstrated rigor, in that order, every time.**
2. **First-principles reframing beats "more features."** Illuminaire (2024 finalist) inverted the premise entirely — instead of Flipkart competing in quick-commerce, they proposed Flipkart *rent out* dark-store infrastructure to its competitors ("sell shovels in a gold rush"). Voldemort inverted "compete with kirana stores" into "partner with kirana stores." Both were praised explicitly for *not* taking the obvious read of the prompt.
3. **Self-awareness of your own weaknesses is rewarded, not penalized.** Ken-spiracy Theorists (2024) won a finals slot specifically for addressing head-on *why Flipkart was late to market* rather than ignoring it. Judges explicitly said they were watching for "a right to win despite tardiness."
4. **Specificity beats scale.** Numbers appear constantly in winning pitches — "195-363 days," "60% of IT budget," "45% of employees over 55," "18 beds freed in first month," "reduce closure time from 30 days to under 1 hour." Vague claims ("faster," "better," "AI-powered") never appear in a winning pitch without a number attached.
5. **A crowded target isn't automatically a losing bet.** CBRE and Narayana Health drew 19 and 18 of the 100 semifinalist teams respectively in 2025 (the most contested targets) — and Narayana Health was also the eventual winning company. Whitespace-seeking is not the same as winning; execution quality within a crowded space can still win. Don't discard "Keeping the machines running" reasoning just because other teams also picked it — win it on execution.
6. **Team composition matters less than voice and precision.** Kenith (2024 finalist, FMS Delhi) was "an English literature graduate, a data scientist, and an app developer" — praised as "technically solid, numerically watertight, and skillfully narrated." Diverse, non-obvious team backgrounds are framed as an asset in the writeup, not a liability, *if* the output is precise.
7. **A live demo/prototype makes the abstract concrete — even when not required.** ArogyaGhar.ai (2025 showcase prototype) uses a consistent pattern worth studying directly: problem stated in plain language → the non-obvious insight stated as a single line ("home recovery is a powerful, shared incentive") → hard evidence cited before the product is even shown (*"19-38% reduction in length of stay, 25% lower costs"*) → then a concrete before/after workflow with real numbers (₹1,24,000 vs ₹1,78,000; 2-3 day manual triage vs AI day-zero assignment) → role-specific testimonials (CMO, CFO, nursing lead each get their own validation). **The structure to copy: problem → insight in one line → evidence → concrete before/after numbers → who specifically benefits and how.**

### 1.4 Direct implications for this team's Q1-Q10 answers

- Q2 (the insight) must be something that could only have come from a real conversation — see §1.2's warning about AI-generated-sounding answers.
- Q3 (six-step loop) and Q5 (rail ask) should each name a specific mechanism, never a technology label alone — per §1.2's "AI/ML" warning.
- Wherever a number can be attached (cost, time, %, count), attach it — per §1.3.4. If real interview data doesn't yet support a number, say so explicitly rather than inventing one (consistent with this project's existing evidence discipline in `system_map.md` and `team_reconciliation.md`).
- Don't be spooked by "Keeping the machines running" being opening #02 of 16 and presumably popular — per §1.3.5, a contested category is not disqualifying.
- If Q9's business-model reasoning touches financials, state real numbers or explicitly flag them as unknown — never hand-wave with jargon, per Deepak Shenoy's quote in §1.2.

## 3. How to think like a systems thinker

*Sources: Sterman's Business Dynamics (Ch.1, Ch.5 read directly), Senge's The Fifth Discipline (opening + archetypes chapters read directly), The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook (Systems Thinking section read directly), Meadows' Thinking in Systems (this project's copy is a 33-page diagram/appendix companion, not the full book — its system-traps and leverage-points appendix is genuine Meadows content, extracted directly).*

**Core mental models**

- **Event vs. feedback view** (Sterman) — most people explain problems as one-off event chains; systems thinkers see themselves *embedded in* a system that reacts to their own actions.
- **Reinforcing vs. balancing loops** (Sterman) — only two loop types exist. Reinforcing loops amplify (the more X, the more Y, the more X); balancing loops self-correct (gap between goal and reality drives corrective action until it closes). "Feedback" isn't praise/criticism — a reinforcing loop can produce a bad outcome (a debt spiral) and a balancing loop a good one (a thermostat).
- **Stocks and flows** (Sterman/Meadows) — a stock accumulates (unresolved tickets, trust in a technician); a flow is the rate that fills/drains it. You cannot change a stock directly, only by changing what flows into or out of it. "Household frustration" is a stock, not an event — it drains through specific flows (resolved incidents) and fills through others (broken promises).
- **Single-loop vs. double-loop learning** (Sterman, citing Argyris) — single-loop fixes a decision without touching the underlying mental model; double-loop changes the mental model itself, which changes the decision rule going forward. Systems thinking is fundamentally a double-loop practice.
- **Mental models are not reality** (Sterman) — his supply-chain example: a company's own diagram of their supply chain had manufacturing dominating it and nobody from procurement, suppliers, or customers in the room — the model wasn't wrong on purpose, it was wrong because of *who was in the room*. Direct parallel: interviewing only households and never technicians/landlords risks the same distortion.
- **The four levels of a systems view** (Fieldbook): Events → Patterns of behavior → Systems (structure) → Mental models — the same "iceberg" most systems-thinking courses teach, illustrated with a worked case (Acme Co. reacted to isolated *events* with isolated fixes for three quarters; only tracking *patterns* over time revealed the real *structure*).
- **Systems archetypes** (Senge) — named, recurring feedback structures. Most relevant here: **Limits to Growth** (a reinforcing loop drives growth until a balancing loop/limiting condition kicks in — the leverage is in the limit, not in pushing harder) and **Shifting the Burden** (a symptomatic fix relieves a problem short-term but removes the pressure to pursue the fundamental fix, and the system becomes addicted to the symptomatic fix while its capacity for the real one atrophies).
- **Meadows' 8 system traps, each with a named "way out"**: Policy Resistance, Tragedy of the Commons, Drift to Low Performance, Escalation, Success to the Successful, Shifting the Burden to the Intervenor, Rule Beating, Seeking the Wrong Goal — a usable checklist, see the framework below.
- **Meadows' 12 leverage points, weakest to strongest**: 12 Numbers (parameters/subsidies) → 11 Buffers → 10 Stock-and-flow structures → 9 Delays → 8 Balancing-loop strength → 7 Reinforcing-loop strength → 6 Information flows → 5 Rules → 4 Self-organization → 3 Goals → 2 Paradigms → 1 Transcending paradigms. **The critical, counterintuitive lesson**: most people's instinct is to intervene at #12 (tweak a number/parameter) because it's easiest — and it's also the weakest. A reminder-app "nudge" is a #12/#11 intervention. Changing *who has access to what information* (#6) or *the rules governing who can act* (#5) is much higher leverage.

**Good practices**

- Ask "what structure produces this behavior?" not "who caused this event?" — reaching to blame a lazy technician or forgetful customer is almost always a sign you're stuck at the event level.
- Draw the causal loop diagram with real notation discipline (Sterman): variable names are nouns, never verbs ("Costs," not "Costs Rise"); every variable has a clear positive direction ("Morale," not "Mental Attitude"); prefer positively-framed variables ("Profit" not "Losses") since negative framing confuses loop polarity; name and number your loops (R1, "the Burnout loop") — naming a loop lets a team point at a shared diagram instead of arguing in words.
- Show delays explicitly — delays are where "worse before better" trade-offs live; giving up on an intervention because it "isn't working yet" is a common, avoidable failure.
- Track patterns over time before reacting to any single event (Acme's second, successful attempt: plot key variables over 3-4 years before proposing a fix).
- Use a shared causal-loop sketch as a team's common language, not a private analysis artifact — the shape communicates even when word choice differs across three people.
- Look for the highest-leverage intervention point, not the easiest one — explicitly resist defaulting to leverage point #12.

**Bad practices / traps**

- Treating "side effects" as a feature of reality instead of a sign your model is too narrow: *"there are no side effects, there are just effects"* (Sterman) — an unintended consequence in the agent design means the system boundary was drawn too small, not bad luck.
- Confusing combinatorial complexity with dynamic complexity — a system can be simple in its rules (a handful of variables) and still produce wildly counterintuitive behavior once feedback and delays are in play. A "simple" household-repair workflow is not therefore predictable.
- Applying a symptomatic fix and mistaking it for a fundamental one — the shifting-the-burden trap. A reminder ping relieves the symptom of forgetting; it does nothing about *why* nobody owns the follow-through.
- Believing your own mental model *is* the system — same failure mode as interviewing only the easiest-to-reach segment and mistaking that partial view for the whole system.
- **Rule beating** — designing a metric that produces the appearance of success while distorting the system (an agent optimized to "reduce reminders sent" instead of "reduce unresolved incidents" — Sricharan's own `report-source.md` independently makes exactly this point: *"Do not count reminders sent or calls placed as customer value."*).
- **Seeking the wrong goal** — confusing effort (calls made) with result (incident actually resolved) is this trap exactly.

**Given-a-problem framework**

1. Start at the event level, but don't stop there — has this exact pattern happened before, to this person or others?
2. Draw the causal loop diagram of the specific mechanism, following the naming rules above; identify at least one reinforcing and one balancing loop if they exist; name and number them.
3. Check the diagram against Meadows' 8 traps — if it matches one, her "way out" for that specific trap is a directly usable design principle, not just a diagnosis.
4. Ask where the delays are — where does "the machine broke" become visible to the party who could actually prevent it, and how late?
5. Locate the intervention on the 12-point leverage scale — resist defaulting to #12; ask explicitly whether this could instead be solved by changing who has access to what information (#6) or what the rules of engagement are (#5).
6. Test small, watch long enough to see delayed effects, then decide — don't commit the whole strategy to the first read of the structure.

**Direct application to this project — new findings, not just theory transfer**

- **The "household as dispatch desk" framing (`system_map.md` §1.1) is a Level-2 pattern, not yet a Level-3 structure.** A candidate reinforcing loop worth testing directly in interviews: *the less reliably a technician follows through → the more the household has to personally verify/chase → the more the household believes "nobody else can be trusted with this" → the less it delegates → the more the technician has to guess at context on arrival → the less reliably the job gets done right the first time → loop closes.* Candidate name: **"the self-fulfilling distrust loop."** If real interviews corroborate this, it's a genuine systems finding, not a restatement of the case brief — exactly what Q2 rewards.
- **Several of the 17 pain-threads in `notes.md` §2 are Shifting-the-Burden candidates**, not isolated failures — threads #5/#6 (commitment enforcement, households just re-calling) are a textbook symptomatic-fix loop. This reframes the product question from "how do we remind people better" to "how do we strengthen the fundamental response and weaken reliance on the symptomatic one."
- **The AMC-vendor-forgetting finding (`system_map.md` §6.4) is a leverage-point question, not just a fact.** If both sides rely on informal memory, the fix might not be reminders (a #12 intervention) — it might be #6, information flow: whoever holds the shared, authoritative record of an appliance's service state becomes structurally load-bearing to both sides at once. This strengthens the Q6 "shared identifier" hypothesis already in `r1.md` beyond what the resource-scan finding alone supported.
- **Run Q5 (the rail to innovate on) through the leverage-point test before locking it**: is "outbound voice that navigates an IVR" (the current leading candidate) a #12/#11 fix (a faster tool for the same low-leverage action — more calls, faster) or does it move #6 (who has access to information — the household's context reaching the provider without repeated re-explanation)? If genuinely the latter, that's a materially stronger argument for Q5 than "this capability doesn't exist yet" alone.

## 4. How to think like a designer

*Sources: Buxton's Sketching User Experiences (core chapters read directly), Desmet & Hekkert's "Framework of Product Experience" (full paper), the beauty/goodness/usability interplay paper (full), a design-thinking-modes chapter, and TOC-level coverage of Tidwell's Designing Interfaces and Hillmann's UX for XR (lower priority for this no-code competition).*

**Core mental models**

- **Problem-setting vs. problem-solving** (Buxton, citing Schön 1983) — the single most load-bearing idea found. "What is the right thing to build?" (problem setting) is a separate job from "is it built well?" (problem solving) — failing either one is failure, even if you nail the other. Tidwell independently states the identical principle: *"Solve the Right Problem, and Then Solve It Right."* Two unrelated authors converging on this is a strong signal it's foundational.
- **The design funnel** (Buxton) — wide at the front (many cheap, disposable sketches exploring divergent concepts), narrowing toward the back (fewer, more refined prototypes answering specific questions). Sketches and prototypes serve *different purposes at different stages* — conflating them is a common failure.
- **Three levels of product experience: aesthetic, meaning, emotional** (Desmet & Hekkert) — usability is not a fourth level, it's a *source* of experience, because usable products more reliably satisfy goals, and goal-satisfaction reads as positive emotion.
- **Pragmatic vs. hedonic quality** (Hassenzahl, via the interplay paper) — pragmatic = achieving *do-goals* (get the AC fixed); hedonic = achieving *be-goals* (feeling competent, in control). Sharpest line found: *"usability itself is of no value. Only facilitating the pursuit of meaningful do-goals helps."* Usability is instrumental, never terminal.
- **Experience changes over time** (Karapanos, Hassenzahl & Martens, via the interplay paper) — week one: usability dominates, beauty tracks *stimulation*. After four weeks: usability recedes, beauty tracks *classiness*, value shifts to ownership/identification. A concept compelling on first demo and one compelling after a month are not solved by the same design decisions.
- **Schemas block reframing** — expertise builds mental shortcuts that speed up familiar problems but actively block novel reframing of unfamiliar ones (the "let the air out of the tires" parable: a child solves a stuck-truck problem specialists couldn't, because he wasn't carrying their schemas). The person closest to a domain is not always best positioned to reframe it — a real risk for a team of PDM students who may over-index on "how service apps are normally designed."

**Good practices**

- Generate quantity before quality, deliberately, at the front of the funnel (the ceramics-class parable: graded on *quantity*, students out-produced the "quality" group in actual quality too, by iterating).
- Match investment to certainty — stay cheap while uncertain, spend once uncertainty falls ("the Gambling Rule").
- Separate problem-setting from problem-solving as distinct, both-required steps — don't let "what should we build" get silently skipped because a prompt already seems to answer it.
- Study one real analogous success in real depth, not just its outcome (Buxton's iPod case: took 3 years and 4 generations to "tip," wasn't first-to-market, depended on ecosystem/business-model tricks and luck — "Apple made a great product" alone would be the wrong lesson).
- Design the product, engineering plan, and business model together, up front — the way film preproduction locks script, budget, cast, and release plan simultaneously.
- Ground hedonic appeal in something task-related, not decorative — a usable-but-tedious concept and an unusable-but-thrilling one can be equally appealing; balance both deliberately.
- Design for the whole lifecycle, not just first contact — what earns trust on day one isn't what sustains engagement on day thirty.

**Bad practices / traps**

- Falling in love with the first idea and skipping divergence.
- Conflating a sketch with a low-fidelity prototype — over-polishing something not yet validated as the right concept.
- Assuming the brief already defines the problem — building competently to a spec that was never interrogated.
- Reductionist views of "joy": usability reductionism ("joy = usable"), design reductionism ("joy = what visual polish adds"), marketing reductionism ("joy = a claim with no substance") — each mistakes one contributing factor for the whole.
- Over-designing novelty without checking adequacy — too much departure from the familiar and users can't tell if the thing even works.
- Letting expertise ossify into schemas that block reframing.
- Presenting only the happy path — nothing in this literature treats a flawless first-use demo as sufficient.

**Given-a-problem framework (1.5-day, no-code sprint)**

1. Set the problem before solving it — spend real time on "what is the right thing to build," separately from "how would it work."
2. Sketch many concepts fast and cheap before committing — words/whiteboard/rough flows count; volume and disposability over polish.
3. Study one real analogous success in depth (this project already has one: IIM Ahmedabad's 2025 "hospital bed-days → AI-orchestrated home days" win — worth studying at Buxton's depth, not just the one-line summary already on file).
4. Once a concept converges, raise your rigor accordingly — don't sketch-quality-check the final Q3 answer, and don't prototype-quality-check Day 1 concepts.
5. Design the "business plan" (Q4-Q9: rails, asset, annexation, bets, incumbent) *with* the six-step loop, not bolted on after.
6. Check the concept against both first-use and sustained-use — does it still make sense after the novelty fades and what matters is trust?
7. Actively counter schema-lock — ask what someone with zero exposure to Urban Company/FSM software would design from scratch.

**Application to Q3 (six-step agent loop, ≤15 words/step)**
Write several six-step versions before picking one — don't submit your first sketch. Each step is its own problem-setting/problem-solving pair: what it should *do* is separate from phrasing it in ≤15 words — don't let the word cap collapse the two. Design "how it knows it's done" around a real customer-defined outcome (machine confirmed usable again), not a vendor's ticket-closed status — per the pragmatic/do-goal distinction above. Treat the step where the agent asks its human as the loop's literal problem-setting checkpoint — the one place the agent has to recognize it doesn't yet know the *right* thing to do, not just execute the next action.

## 5. How to think like a UX / user researcher

*Sources: Kuniavsky's Observing the User Experience, Erika Hall's Just Enough Research, Constantine's Users/Roles/Personas, Sharp/Preece/Rogers' Interaction Design, Boellstorff et al.'s ethnography handbook, Dumas & Loring's Moderating Usability Tests, Gavin Lew & Schumacher's AI and UX (all read directly at TOC + core-chapter depth); Becker's Learn HCI and Carroll's HCI in the New Millennium synthesized from general knowledge given time constraints, flagged rather than hidden. This is the most time-critical section — real interviews start today.*

**Core mental models**

- **Say vs. do** (Kuniavsky) — the single most load-bearing distinction in this whole discipline. What people say they do/want/would do is systematically unreliable; what they actually did in one specific real incident is the real signal. Already the operating principle behind both this project's and Sricharan's interview guides.
- **Right-sized research** (Erika Hall) — "just enough" means matching rigor/volume to the actual decision it informs, not to an abstract standard of completeness. A 3-6 interview sprint is not a compromised version of "real" research — it's the correctly-sized research for a 2-day decision window, as long as its limits are stated honestly.
- **Emic vs. etic** (Boellstorff et al.) — emic = the participant's own words and categories ("duplicate RAM," "jugaad"); etic = the researcher's analytic categories ("commitment enforcement failure"). Record the participant's own words before translating into your framework, so you can check the framework against the words later.
- **Personas/roles as compressed hypotheses, not facts** (Constantine) — personas and user roles are both **models**, built to be useful for a specific decision, not empirical findings. This project's candidate segments (ICP A-E) are role-hypotheses in this sense — useful for organizing recruitment, not something one interview can "confirm," only fail to contradict.
- **Moderator neutrality as a learnable discipline, not a personality trait** (Dumas & Loring) — their "ten golden rules of moderating" are concrete, learnable behaviors, directly useful for a 3-person team where not everyone has run interviews before.
- **AI is not exempt from UX discipline** (Lew & Schumacher) — AI products fail for the same UX reasons non-AI products fail: a single bad experience poisons trust in the whole category, and benefit-of-the-doubt is a limited, spendable resource. Directly relevant to designing the agent's failure-recovery paths — a no-show handled badly won't just lose that interaction, it poisons the whole concept of delegating to an agent.

**Good practices**

- Interview about one specific, recent, real incident — never general habits or opinions.
- Distinguish said / observed / artifact-supported / inferred / unknown for every claim (already Sricharan's own discipline in `Fieldwork_Templates.md` — genuinely correct practice, not overkill).
- Follow the story chronologically before imposing categories — "what happened first, then what" before asking about roles/money/authority.
- Ask for artifacts, not just accounts — a photo, a thread, an invoice, as a check against the fallibility and self-flattery of verbal recall.
- Neutral, specific follow-ups — "can you give a specific example," "what happened then," "is that remembered or shown in a record" — never "why didn't you just..." framings that presuppose judgment.
- Recruit for a negative/smooth case on purpose, not just painful ones — stress-tests whether a hypothesized mechanism is real or a sampling artifact.
- Debrief immediately, alone, before group discussion — capture your own interpretation and rival explanations while fresh, before groupthink sets in.
- Informed consent is a process, not a form — explain what happens to the material, ask separately about each use, treat "I didn't fully understand" as a stop condition.

**Bad practices / traps**

- Asking about hypothetical future behavior ("would you use an assistant for this?") instead of reconstructing actual past behavior — the single most-repeated warning across every one of these books.
- Leading or compound questions — bundling two concepts into one question makes the answer uninterpretable.
- Treating a single vivid anecdote as a finding — a sharp quote is one data point; without independent corroboration it's an anecdote, not an insight. This is exactly why this project's convergence protocol requires independent corroboration before locking Q2.
- Confirmation bias in synthesis — unconsciously steering toward evidence for a belief you already hold. Antidote: name a rival explanation for every hypothesis before testing it.
- Researcher-as-expert positioning — presenting yourself as already knowing what's wrong, which makes participants perform agreement instead of describing real experience.
- Skipping the debrief because you're short on time — exactly the failure mode a 2-day sprint risks; most information gets silently lost here, not during the interview itself.
- A photo/artifact that proves the *object's* condition, not the *mechanism* — a photo of a broken machine proves it's broken, not why coordination around it was hard.

**Given-a-problem framework**

1. State the decision the research needs to inform before writing a single question ("what should Q2's insight be, and what does it change in the agent design").
2. Write screening/eligibility criteria first (already done: coordinated a household repair/service incident in the last 90 days).
3. Draft the interview as chronological reconstruction, not a topic list — trigger → first action → escalations → resolution, with authority/money/logistics questions woven in where they'd naturally arise in the story.
4. Pilot on 1-2 people first, explicitly to catch confusing wording; label pilot data separately.
5. Debrief solo within 10 minutes of each session, tagging every claim said/observed/artifact/inferred/unknown.
6. Only after 3+ sessions, synthesize as a group — lay out incidents, not conclusions; group by mechanism; look for independent corroboration before naming an insight.
7. Actively hunt for the disconfirming case — one more interview or probe aimed at the strongest rival explanation.

**5 things to do differently in your next interview, starting today**

1. Never ask "would you use an assistant for this" — ask "tell me about the last time this happened," full stop.
2. After any answer that sounds like an opinion, ask "can you give me a specific example of that."
3. Write down the participant's own words ("duplicate RAM," "jugaad") before translating into your own framework — paraphrasing too early loses real signal.
4. The moment an interview ends, before talking to anyone else, write your own 10-minute solo debrief — said, observed, inferred, and what would prove you wrong.
5. Deliberately interview at least one person whose recent experience was easy or smooth — otherwise you can't tell if your mechanism is real or just a property of who you talked to.

## 6. How to think like an experiment / survey designer

*Sources: Cozby & Bates' Methods in Behavioral Research (13th ed.) and King/Churchill/Tan's Designing with Data. This section doubles as a direct, line-by-line audit of the team's own S0-S30 survey already drafted in `research/report-source.md` Pages 10-12 — with a decision now that survey rollout is confirmed (per this session's interview-volume decision).*

**Core mental models**

- **Construct validity vs. reliability** — whether you're measuring the thing you think you're measuring (validity) is a separate question from whether the measure is consistent (reliability). A perfectly reliable measure can still measure the wrong concept.
- **Response bias types**: social desirability (underreporting undesirable behavior), acquiescence/yea-saying (agreeing with everything regardless of content), interviewer bias, recall bias (why "within the past 90 days" framing exists at all).
- **Convenience sample vs. representative sample** — a convenience sample is legitimate and extremely common in real research, and licenses *relationship/mechanism* claims ("X co-occurs with Y in this group") but never *population-prevalence* claims ("X% of Indian households..."). Most published research uses convenience samples; this is fine, provided the limitation is disclosed and no false-precision claim is smuggled in.
- **The hypothesis-construction frame** (King/Churchill/Tan): *"For [user group], if [change] then [effect] because [rationale], which will impact [measure]."* Every clause is a discipline check — no user group means you can't recruit correctly, no rationale means you can't distinguish a confirming result from a lucky one, no measure means the hypothesis can never actually be tested.
- **Question-order effects** — the same underlying attitude produces different answers depending on what was asked immediately before it. Fix is structural: ask open/unprimed questions before structured/list questions on the same topic, and ask about a concrete recent incident before asking for evaluations.

**Good practices**

- Tie every question to an explicit research objective before writing it — "what will I do differently once I have this answer" is the filter for whether a question belongs.
- Ask the most important/interesting questions first, demographics last (maximizes completion rates).
- Offer "cannot recall"/"not applicable"/"do not know" wherever a real respondent could legitimately lack that information — forcing a false answer is worse than a missing one.
- Pilot test with think-aloud interviews ("what did this question mean to you?") before launch — this is where ambiguous wording actually gets caught, not in review.
- Keep response-scale format consistent within one instrument.
- State the sampling method and its limits explicitly wherever results are reported — a convenience sample described honestly is more credible than one dressed up with false-precision statistics.
- Go broad first when generating candidate hypotheses, narrow later — narrowing too early is the more common failure.
- Prioritize which hypothesis to pursue with a cheap gut-check on potential impact before investing in a full study.

**Bad practices / traps**

- Double-barreled questions — two concepts in one question produce uninterpretable answers when a respondent agrees with one half and not the other.
- Loaded/leading wording — emotionally charged words measure the respondent's reaction to the word, not the underlying attitude.
- Negative-wording confusion — "should X NOT happen" inverts the meaning of agreement and reliably produces response errors.
- Memory overload — long, multi-clause questions collapse under their own complexity.
- **Treating a convenience sample as representative** — the single most common misuse in student research; computing a margin of error or claiming "X% of Indian households" from a friend-network sample is a real error, not just a caveat to add later.
- Confirmation bias in hypothesis selection — looking only for data that supports the hypothesis you already like.
- Confusing stated preference with demonstrated behavior — a survey captures what people *say* they'd want, never what they'd actually do.

**Given-a-problem framework**

1. Write down the exact decision this data will change — if no decision changes based on the answer, cut the question.
2. Draft in order: incident/behavior questions first (unprimed, open before closed), then evaluative/attitude questions, then demographics last.
3. For every closed question: is it single-barreled? Neutral wording? Does "cannot recall/NA" exist where needed?
4. Run 2-3 think-aloud pilots and fix wording — do not skip this even under time pressure, it's the cheapest error-catching step available.
5. Decide the analysis plan (denominators, exclusions, what counts as "unexpected") *before* seeing results.
6. State the sampling method and its limits in one sentence that actually gets published, not buried.

## 7. Guardrails that apply across all four disciplines and the competition itself

- **Never fabricate evidence.** The competition's own terms state fabricated evidence is explicitly disqualifying (`research/report-source.md` Page 6). This is not just an ethical default — it's a stated rule.
- **Label every claim by its evidence tier.** This project already uses `[CASE]`/`[LAW]`/`[TEAM]`/`[HYPOTHESIS]`/`[NEEDS INTERVIEW]` tags (`system_map.md` §0) — keep using them as real interviews come in, upgrading tags as evidence arrives rather than writing new untagged claims.
- **A technology name is not a mechanism.** Say what it specifically does, per §1.2.
- **A financial claim without a real number, or an explicit "unknown," is a red flag to judges** — per §1.2's Deepak Shenoy quote.
- **Boldness needs a rigor partner, always** — per §1.3.1.
