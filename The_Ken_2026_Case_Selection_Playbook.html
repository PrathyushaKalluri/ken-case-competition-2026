Scoring logic

Stage 0 — fatal-flaw gate

Can the agent observe enough context to act?
Can it decide within bounded rules rather than hallucinate high-stakes judgement?
Can the user legally and practically grant the required authority?
Can it act through available rails or realistic interfaces?
Can it verify completion?
Can it recover when a third party fails, no-shows or rejects?
Would the product still create value if AI is imperfect?
Can the team demonstrate the full loop credibly?

Stage 1 — Agent + insight fit 40% of base

Voice rail · 10%
Does phone/voice eliminate real coordination work?
Payments / authorisation · 10%
Can bounded authority move money or approve a meaningful step?
Logistics rail · 10%
Does physical movement, pickup or delivery materially matter?
New insight potential · 25%
Can research expose a surprising mechanism beyond the prompt?
Emotional / consequential depth · 20%
Is the pain intense enough to delegate, pay or switch?
Agent actionability · 25%
Can the agent complete work rather than advise or remind?

Stage 3 — Win conditions 35% of base

Primary-research access · 25%
Evidence is explicitly judged; recruitment quality can decide the case.
Right to win / prototypability · 15%
Can the team learn and build enough to make the solution credible?
Headroom · 15%
Does the wedge expand into a meaningful business or system?
Uncrowded / differentiation · 15%
Will judges see a fresh insight rather than a commodity AI wrapper?
Feasibility / ecosystem control · 20%
How much success depends on integrations or counterparties you do not control?
Narrative clarity · 10%
Can the thesis and demo be understood in about 60–90 seconds?

Stage 2 — Behavior quality 25% of base

1. Repetition — highest priority
Repeated execution creates retention, learning loops and measurable time saved.
2. Frustration — medium-high priority
Frustration increases willingness to delegate, switch and pay.
3. Procrastination dependence — penalty / low priority
An agent can remove administrative friction but cannot reliably manufacture motivation. A high-procrastination problem must show a controllable mechanism, not just reminders.

Base score

40% Stage 1 + 25% Stage 2 + 35% Stage 3. Final case selection then applies explicit judgement for evidence quality, genericity, sample size, third-party control, regulation, episodic usage and the quality of the emerging second-order insight.

What previous winners and finalists reveal

The useful pattern is not “copy their solution.” It is how they framed the system, found a blind spot, bounded AI authority and communicated the strategy.

2025 winner — A-Team / Narayana HealthShift expensive hospital-bed days into data-rich, AI-orchestrated home days, packaged into a guaranteed-price treatment episode. The pattern is system-level thinking: alternatives, incentives, dependencies and economics — not a narrow feature.
2025 finalist — FinVaani / ZerodhaFound an incumbent blind spot created by Zerodha’s own strength: self-serve power-user design leaves guided first-timers under-served. They bounded AI authority carefully: form prefilling and intent detection, but not order execution or financial advice.
2025 finalist — Vācaka.AIAvoided generic voice synthesis and focused on cultural and linguistic localisation depth, incumbent blind spots, defensibility and what current voice AI misses.
2024 consistency check — BigBasketThe winning direction leaned into company-specific strengths rather than generic quick-commerce. Strong system awareness plus a memorable, simple narrative repeated as a pattern.

Winner-pattern checklist for 2026

  • Start from observed human pain, not an AI capability.
  • Find a second-order insight that the prompt does not already hand you.
  • Map alternatives, incentives, blockers, dependencies and failure modes.
  • Use an incumbent or system strength as the blind spot when possible.
  • Bound agent authority precisely — state what it will not do.
  • Use rails as execution primitives, not decorative features.
  • Design observe → decide → act → verify → recover.
  • Demo action, not a chatbot.
  • Have one memorable thesis with simple economics and impact metrics.
  • Prefer controllable and applicable solutions over impressive but dependency-heavy concepts.
Competition thesis: the competition starts after the supplied problem statement. Restating the prompt and adding “AI agent” is unlikely to stand out; your defensibility comes from what your interviews discover that the survey did not.

Primary-research plan

Do not ask “Would you use an AI agent?”

Reconstruct behavior before showing a solution. The goal is evidence and unexpected findings, not concept-validation theatre.

Recency & repetition

  • When was the last time this happened?
  • What happened the time before?
  • How many times in the last 90 days?

Actual work

  • What did you physically do, in order?
  • How many calls, apps and people were involved?
  • How much time elapsed vs active effort?

Consequence

  • What happened because you did it late or got it wrong?
  • What was the monetary, time or emotional cost?

Artifacts & workaround

  • Show the WhatsApp thread, spreadsheet, invoice, screenshot, note or calendar.
  • Who else became part of the workaround?

Delegatability & authority

  • Which part would you pay another human to do?
  • What could they safely do without asking?
  • At what point must they come back to you?

Rails & willingness to pay

  • Who did you call?
  • Where did money or approval move?
  • What physically moved or had to be picked up?
  • What have you already paid, outsourced or sacrificed to avoid this?

Two-round discovery funnel

Round 1: 5–8 interviews for each of the five discovery candidates (~25–40 interviews). Objective: kill weak mechanisms quickly.
Eliminate three: use observed recurrence, delegatability, authority, artifact evidence, controllability and quality of the unexpected insight.
Round 2: 15–20 deep interviews across the final two, including counterparty interviews where relevant.
Final choice: pick the case with the strongest evidence-backed second-order thesis that still passes agentability and feasibility gates.

Five hypotheses worth testing first

Machines

The dominant pain is not finding a technician; it is commitment recovery after a service promise is made.

School

Parents do not need fewer messages; they need unstructured school communications converted into owned household commitments.

Wedding

Vendor discovery is not the hard part; enforceable commitments, milestones and payment leverage are.

Rent

The unmet market begins after the rental agreement, when platforms and brokers disappear but coordination continues.

Pet

The moat is passive longitudinal history creation from actions, not a pet-record locker.

What a winning submission should contain

1. One-line human truth
A memorable sentence from observed behavior, not “users face inconvenience.”
2. Evidence
Interview sample, artifacts, last-occurrence journeys, quotes and the surprising thing you were not looking for.
3. Second-order insight
Explain why the obvious problem statement is incomplete or wrong.
4. System map
User, counterparty, alternatives, incentives, dependencies, risks and value flows.
5. Agent contract
What it observes, decides, acts on, verifies and recovers from — and what it will not do.
6. Rails
Show why voice, payments/auth and logistics are necessary; do not bolt them on.
7. 90-second end-to-end demo
A real job disappears. Include the exception path, no-show or rejection.
8. Economics / measurable value
Time saved, failure reduced, money recovered/controlled, completion rate and repeat usage.
9. Feasibility / failure modes
Dependencies, consent, privacy, human fallback and recovery.
10. Wedge → headroom
Why the narrow entry point can expand without losing strategic coherence.
