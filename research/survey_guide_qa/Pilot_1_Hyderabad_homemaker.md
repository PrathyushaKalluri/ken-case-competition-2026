# Cognitive pilot 1 of 2 — Hyderabad homemaker (Lakshmi persona)

Run against survey_v1.md using the standard cognitive-pretesting method (what did this question mean to you, how did you work out your answer, was an option missing) already prescribed in the team's own research plan. Produced by a general-purpose AI agent instructed to answer the whole draft in character, then critique it. Directly informed the v2 fixes in Survey_Plain_Language_v2.md and the final Word document.

**Persona**: Lakshmi, 46, homemaker, Hyderabad. Ordinary 150-flat gated community (not a premium managed one), husband works long hours at a bank, one college-going son. Handles almost all household coordination. Comfortable with WhatsApp, not deeply tech-savvy. Functional but not fluent English, thinks partly in Telugu. Never used a service app; always calls a known local technician found via word of mouth. Real incident used for the pilot: washing machine stopped spinning properly ~3 weeks ago (worn belt), same technician came the next day, ₹450, paid cash after a quick phone check with her husband on whether the price sounded fair.

# Step 1 — Question-by-question walkthrough (as Lakshmi)

**Form description**
(a) Understood: students doing a study, anonymous, no selling. (b) The "we will never ask for money, OTPs, or bank details" line is actually reassuring — good instinct, keep it. (c) No answer needed here. (d) No leading/double-barreled issue. One small snag: "case-competition submission (The Ken 2026)... seen by organisers and partners" — "partners" is vague to her and could quietly revive her suspicion ("partners" sounds like companies, is this an ad thing after all?). Minor, not fatal.

**Q1** Consent — Yes/No. Fine, clear, no issue.

**Q2** Age 18+. Fine. She'd answer Yes.

**Q3** City. Fine, picks Hyderabad.

**Q4** Apartment community name, optional. (b) Slight hesitation — "why do they need my building name, even if optional" — mild privacy twitch, but the parenthetical explanation helps. She'd probably still skip it or type it vaguely ("XYZ enclave"). Fine as is.

**Q5** Who deals with repairs. She picks "I usually do it myself." No confusion.

**Q6** "Personally involved... fixed, serviced, or newly installed... in the last 3 months" — she answers Yes (washing machine, 3 weeks ago). (b) Slightly long sentence but manageable. No real confusion.

**Q7** Which appliance — Washing machine. Fine.

**Q8** Why did you need someone — "It stopped working." Fine, matches her case well (noise + stopped spinning).

**Q9** When — "2-4 weeks ago" (3 weeks). Fine, clean bucket.

**Q10** Whose appliance — "Mine" (well, household's — she'd read "Mine" as "our family's," which is fine, that's how Indian respondents generally read "mine" for shared household property). No issue.

**Q11** Does building have maintenance office for appliance repairs. She'd answer "No, everyone arranges it themselves" — accurate for a 150-flat ordinary complex, not a premium managed one. Fine, no confusion.

**Q12** How did she get in touch — "Called a technician I already knew." Fits her exactly (found originally via neighbor, has used him before). Good.

**Q13** Open text on what happened. She might write a line or skip it (optional). Fine.

**Q14** How many more times did you call/message after first reaching out. Her case: technician came the very next day, one call, done. Answer: "None." Fine, matches.

**Q15** Minutes personally spent on calls/messages/looking things up (not counting repair time). (a) She'd think: the one call to the technician, plus the call to her husband to check if ₹450 was fair — maybe 5-10 minutes total. (b) This is a slightly awkward mental-math question for a non-numeric-minded respondent — she'd probably guess "10." Not a huge problem but be aware some respondents will find "estimate your minutes" oddly precise-sounding for something so quick. Not broken, just a spot where some respondents type "0" meaning "hardly any time" rather than a real number — worth having reassurance text like "even a rough guess is fine, e.g. 5, 10, 30."

**Q16** Current situation — "Fully fixed and working." Fine, matches.

**Q17** (shown because fixed) — time from noticing to fixed. Her case: noticed the noise, it got worse over what seems like a short window, called the next day, fixed same visit. If we take "noticed problem" = whenever the noise started, this could be "1-2 days" or even "same day" depending how she mentally times it (does "noticed" mean when it first started making noise, or when it actually stopped spinning and she decided to call?). (b) **Mild ambiguity**: "noticed the problem" vs. "decided to call" could be different moments for a fault that develops gradually (noise → worse noise → stops spinning). Not fatal, but worth a small clarifying phrase.

**Q18** Overall hassle — she'd say "A little" or "Very little" — it was actually a smooth experience. Fine, no issue, good scale.

**Q19** Who had final say on spending — her case: she called her husband to ask if the price was fair before paying. (a) She'd have to think about this one: technically she made the call and decided to go ahead with the repair before even knowing the exact price (technician diagnosed it, fixed it, then told her the cost) — the "final say to go ahead and spend" happened almost automatically since it was a small already-known technician doing an obviously needed fix; the price-check call to husband happened *after* the work was basically done, more as a sanity check before paying than as permission to proceed. (b) **This is where a subtle double-barreled problem shows up**: "final say on whether to go ahead" conflates two different decisions — (1) deciding to call the technician / let him proceed with repair, and (2) approving the final price. In her real story these are answered by different logic (she decided to proceed herself since it was obviously needed; but she checked with husband specifically on the price). The form only lets her pick one person for one merged decision. She'd likely just pick "We decided together" as a hedge, which is technically true but loses the more precise story — a legit case-comp researcher would care about this distinction (who decides *to* fix vs. who approves *the cost*). (c) missing nuance, not missing option exactly — option list itself is fine (Me/household/landlord/office/together/no decision/not sure), it's the question's scope that quietly bundles two decisions.

**Q20** Who paid — "Me" (she paid cash on the spot). Fine, clean.

**Q21** Did price change after agreed — in her case no price was agreed in advance at all; the technician just told her ₹450 after finishing. (b) This is actually **exactly the option "No price was agreed in advance"** — good, that option is there and she'd pick it correctly. This design already anticipated her exact situation, good catch by whoever wrote it.

**Q21a** Skipped since her price didn't change.

**Q22** Was any part sent elsewhere — "No" (technician had the belt with him). Fine.

**Q22a** Skipped.

**Q23** Do you keep notes/list/sticker/WhatsApp chat for servicing dates or technician numbers. (a) She'd think about this — she probably just saves the technician's number in her phone contacts. (b) **Missing option / ambiguity**: "saved his number in my phone" isn't explicitly a "note, list, notebook, sticker, or WhatsApp chat" — is a plain phone contact save covered under "Yes, something like that," or does that phrase imply something more deliberate like a notebook/sticker system? She might genuinely hesitate and either pick "No, I don't" (because she doesn't keep a system, just has his number saved, which she doesn't think of as "keeping a note") or pick "I just remember it in my head" (also technically wrong — the number is in her phone, not her head). This is a real gap: **saving a contact in your phone is probably the single most common "system" ordinary people actually use, and it doesn't map cleanly onto any of the three given options.**

**Q24** AMC or call someone you know. (b) **"AMC" is exactly the acronym flagged as a concern** — even though the question explains it in parentheses ("sometimes called an AMC"), a non-fluent-English, non-tech-savvy respondent may not know what a "yearly paid contract" itself means in this context either — is that like an insurance policy? A subscription? The parenthetical gloss for AMC is good practice, but "yearly paid contract" is still a slightly formal/legal-sounding phrase for spoken-register English. She'd answer "No, I just call someone I know" correctly, but this question earns extra scrutiny below.

**Q25** Reminders for other things — vehicle insurance, **PUC**, gas cylinder booking. (b) **"PUC" is a hard acronym failure.** PUC = Pollution Under Control certificate for vehicles. Lakshmi doesn't drive/handle the vehicle paperwork (her husband likely does), and even if she's heard the term, it's not spelled out at all here (unlike AMC, which at least got a parenthetical explanation). She would either skip this (it's optional, so likely outcome) or feel a flash of "I don't know what that is" which very mildly undermines her confidence in the whole survey ("what is this app trying to test me on"). This is the single clearest jargon fix needed.

**Q26–Q29** Open-ended reflection questions, all optional. (a) Understood as invitations to elaborate. (b) No jargon issues. By this point (roughly Q26 of 33), fatigue is a real risk — four consecutive open paragraph questions in a row (26, 27, 28, 29) is a lot to ask even as "optional," and a respondent who has already answered everything dutifully up to here may just click through without reading them properly, or abandon before reaching them.

**Q30** Age group. Fine, matches her demographic bucket 45-54.

**Q31** Gender. Fine, has "would rather not say" and self-describe option — appropriately inclusive, not intrusive.

**Q32** Home ownership type. Fine — "Own home" matches (assuming she owns).

**Q33** Who handles repairs in household. **(d) This is a near-exact repeat of Q5** ("who usually deals with getting things repaired") asked 28 questions earlier. Lakshmi, filling this out honestly, would think "didn't I already answer this?" — mild irritation/confusion at the end of a long form. This is a genuine repetition flag.

**Closing screen** Fine, no pressure, contact is opt-in.

# Step 2 — Specific flags

**Acronyms/jargon:**
- **PUC (Q25)** — not explained at all. Hard fail for comprehension. Fix needed.
- **AMC (Q24)** — explained via parenthetical, but "yearly paid contract" is itself a slightly stiff phrase. Borderline OK but can be made more natural/spoken-register.
- No other acronyms found (AC, WhatsApp are fine — universally understood in this context).

**Where her real answer doesn't fit the options:**
- **Q23** — "saved his number in my phone contacts" doesn't clearly map to "Yes, something like that" / "No, I don't" / "I just remember it in my head." Needs an explicit phone-contacts option.
- **Q19** — double-barreled: "who decided to proceed" vs. "who approved the final price" are different people/moments in her real story (she decided herself, but her husband effectively co-signed the price). Only one merged answer is possible.
- **Q15** — not broken, but no explicit reassurance that a rough/small guess (e.g., "5 minutes") is a valid, useful answer.
- **Q17** — mild ambiguity between "noticed the problem" (when noise started, could be gradual) vs. "when it was actually working again."

**Repetitive questions:**
- **Q33 repeats Q5** almost word-for-word. One of these should be cut.

**Tone — cold/corporate/scam-like anywhere?**
No. The tone throughout is warm, plain, and reassuring — a real strength of the draft.

**Does it take too long / ask too much before letting her talk in her own words?**
Partially yes — the bulk of true "in your own words" richness is backloaded into Q26-29, four paragraph questions in a row at the very end.

# Step 3 — Proposed rewrites

**Q15 — add reassurance:** "A rough guess is fine — even '5' or '10' minutes is a useful answer." *Short answer, numeric, with "Don't remember / Hard to say" as an explicit option.*

**Q17 — clarify the start point:** "From when you first noticed something was wrong (even before you decided to call anyone) to when it was actually working again, how long did it take?"

**Q19 — split the double-barreled question into two:**
Q19a. Who decided to go ahead and get it fixed (call someone / let the technician start work)?
Q19b. If the cost came up for discussion, who had the final say on whether the price was OK?

**Q23 — add the missing "saved in phone" option:** "Yes — saved contact/number in my phone / Yes — a note, list, or notebook / Yes — something else / No, I don't keep track of it / I just remember it in my head"

**Q24 — plainer phrasing:** "Do you pay a yearly fee to a company or brand for regular servicing of this appliance (sometimes called an 'AMC' or annual maintenance contract), or do you just call someone you know whenever something breaks?"

**Q25 — fix PUC:** "...your vehicle's pollution certificate (PUC)..." — spell out "pollution certificate" before the acronym.

**Q33 — cut it entirely; Q5 already captures this.**

**Section 6 open-ended block — reduce backloading fatigue:** move "what was the most difficult part" earlier, right after the hassle question, and add a framing sentence before the remaining open block.

# Step 4 — Realistic time and drop-off estimate

Total realistic time if she completes everything properly: 8-10 minutes, running over the promised "5-7 minutes," mostly because of the four-paragraph stretch at the end and two extra beats of hesitation (Q19, Q23). She'd likely push through to the end given a positive incident story, but skip most of Q26-29. A less patient respondent from the same WhatsApp group is a real dropout risk at Q23-25 (the AMC/PUC stretch) or the Q26-29 paragraph block, most likely right at the transition into that block.

---

# Step 5 — Blunt verdict

Close but not ready to send as-is. The tone, structure, and screening logic are genuinely well-built. But PUC is unexplained jargon that will confuse or embarrass non-driving housewife respondents, Q23's options don't cover the single most common tracking method (saved phone contacts), Q19 quietly asks two different questions as one and will produce mushy "we decided together" answers that hide the real decision-split the researchers want, and Q33 duplicates Q5 closely enough to annoy anyone paying attention at the end. All one-line fixes, not a redesign — but on a self-administered form with no interviewer to clarify, jargon and ambiguity like this don't get asked about, they just get skipped, guessed at, or cause silent abandonment.
