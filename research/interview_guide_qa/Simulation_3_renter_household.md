# Step 2 — Question-by-question critique

**Section 0 (ground rules):** Fine, well-designed. But it's generic — it doesn't flag Section F specifically as the highest-risk moment for sympathy-fishing drift, even though F is explicitly called "the most important section." Recommend an inline reminder there (see Step 3).

**Section B:** All fine as written. "Who else got involved, at any point?" is appropriately neutral — it's what let the landlord thread emerge without leading. **Not flagged.**

**Section C:** All fine. The WhatsApp-chat probe is genuinely well-built for this persona — specific enough to be answerable, not phrased as "do you have a system" (jargon), and explicitly optional ("would you be comfortable"). **Not flagged.**

**Section D:** Fine, plainly behavioral throughout. **Not flagged.**

**Section E:** Fine. **Not flagged.**

**Section F — main risk area:**
- "Was there any point where it wasn't clear what should happen next...?" — fine, neutral.
- "What happened while you were waiting?" — this is the single best-designed question in the guide for this persona; it's how the 3-day delay surfaced without ever asking "was that frustrating." **Not flagged.**
- "Did you need anyone's permission before this repair could happen — like a landlord, a family elder, or a building office?" — **minor flag.** "Permission" carries a faint parent-child connotation for an adult tenant, which could read as slightly infantilizing on a bad day. Low risk, but an easy improvement:
  - *Rewrite:* "Did you need to check with anyone before you could go ahead with this — a landlord, a family elder, or a building office?"
- "How did you ask them, and how long did it take to hear back?" — good, factual, but has a *scope gap*, not a bias problem (see Step 4): "hear back" is ambiguous about whether it covers only the initial request or the whole reimbursement chase. Not leading, just incomplete. Suggest adding one more explicit follow-up:
  - *Add:* "Once you told them about it after the fact, how long did it take to actually sort out (get paid back, adjusted, etc.)? Was that straightforward or did you have to follow up?"
- "Would that landlord/person be open to a short 10-minute conversation with us...? A yes or no is completely fine." — excellent, low-pressure framing. **Not flagged.**

**Section G:** Fine, appropriately skippable. **Not flagged.**

**Section H:** Fine, all behavioral/factual. **Not flagged.**

**Section I:**
- "Was there one part that was the most difficult or the most annoying? Doesn't have to be a big thing." — mostly fine, but unlike Section F, it lacks the explicit permission-to-say-"nothing was hard" that F has ("If they say everything was clear... that's a real, useful answer too. Don't push for a problem that isn't there."). For a guarded respondent this asymmetry matters: the phrasing already presupposes something was annoying. **Flag — add parity language:**
  - *Add after the main question:* "(If honestly nothing stood out as particularly hard, that's a completely valid answer — don't push for one.)"
- "Was there a moment where you just decided to leave it and deal with it later? What was going on then, that made you put it off?" — this is the question that surfaced the tap. It's well phrased (no blame, "avoid: never ask why didn't you just do X" is already built in). **Not flagged**, but see Step 5 for a structural/placement concern, not a wording one.

**Section J:** The hypothetical-then-grounding pairing is exactly correct Mom Test discipline and produced a real precedent (the friend/tap). **Not flagged** on wording — flagged on placement/survivability in Step 4/5.

**Landlord mini-addendum:** All four questions are behavioral and neutral. **Not flagged.**

**The one real problem in Step 1 wasn't in the guide text — it was the interviewer's off-script ad-lib** ("that must be so annoying"). That's the exact failure mode the brief warned about, and it happened *despite* good guide wording, purely from natural rapport-building instinct. This is worth calling out because it means the guide's general Section 0 warning isn't sufficient insurance — it's stated once, upfront, and easy to forget 20 minutes in when a respondent says something that invites a sympathetic reflex.

# Step 3 — Does the renter block + "hardest part"/"leave for later" questions actually stay neutral?

**Yes, as written on the page, both hold up.** Delivered exactly as scripted, they got the real approval-delay story (Section F) and the real unreported tap (Section I) without ever sounding like they were fishing for a complaint about Mr. Reddy. The specific mechanism that works: they ask "what happened" and "how long," never "was that fair/hard/annoying-of-them." That discipline is real and it's why Priya, who is specifically primed to clam up on landlord friction, still delivered the full incident.

**The actual risk is not in the printed question — it's in the gap around the question**, specifically:
1. Right after "he didn't reply for three days" (Section F) or "I had to chase him twice" — this is the single highest-temptation moment in the whole interview for an interviewer to insert an unscripted sympathetic aside. The guide's general ground rules cover this in the abstract but there's no inline flag at the exact trigger point.
2. Section I's main question ("most difficult or annoying part") lacks the same "a non-answer is a valid answer" permission that Section F explicitly has, which subtly nudges toward performing a complaint.

**Fixes:**
- Insert directly under the renter-block bullets in Section F: *"(High-risk moment: resist any urge to sympathize or editorialize here — no 'that's so slow of him' or 'that must have been frustrating.' Just ask the next question on the list.)"*
- Add the parity line to Section I noted in Step 2.

# Step 5 — Timing sanity check: does the tap incident fit naturally, or does it need its own prompt?

**It needs closer protection than it currently has.** Section B's frame — "the last time something needed a repair, a service, or had to be newly installed... tell me the whole story" — is anchored to a single, resolved, technician-mediated incident. The tap issue is definitionally excluded from that frame: it was never serviced by a technician, never became a "story" with a professional-intervention ending, and isn't "the last time" (the AC is more recent). A respondent's memory for informally-resolved, never-escalated problems is weak by nature — it doesn't come up unless something specifically cues it.

The guide *does* have a designed hook for exactly this — Section I's "was there a moment where you just decided to leave it and deal with it later?" — and that's a genuinely clever, non-obvious design choice; give the guide credit for it. But it's fragile for three compounding reasons:
1. **Section I is explicitly the first section to drop under time pressure** ("If you're running short, drop from Section I first").
2. **It arrives 33+ minutes in**, when both interviewer and respondent are likely fatigued and running long (Section F, the "never drop" section, is exactly the kind of section that overruns with a talkative respondent — as it did here).
3. **The prompt is indirect** — "leave it and deal with it later" is anchored to "looking back at the whole thing" (the AC story), not framed as "was there ever anything else, a totally separate issue, you never even reported." It worked here because Priya voluntarily jumped to a different incident, which the question technically permits but doesn't explicitly invite.

**Recommendation:** Don't rely on Section I alone for this. Either:
- (a) Explicitly protect the "leave it for later" sub-question specifically (not all of Section I) from being dropped, the way B/C/F are protected, or
- (b) Add one short, explicitly protected question earlier (Section C is a natural home, right after the WhatsApp-artifact probe): *"Has there ever been something small in the house that needed fixing that you just never got around to calling anyone about, or dealt with some other way?"* This removes the dependency on time budget and on the "leave it" question's indirect phrasing, and would independently corroborate whatever Section I gets.

The same logic applies, doubly, to Section J and the delegation precedent — it's the last section, explicitly droppable, and now depends on I having already surfaced the tap. If a team is running short and drops I, they lose both the tap AND (most likely) the delegation grounding, since Priya's real delegation precedent is anchored to the same tap story.

# Step 6 — Overall verdict

The guide's language is genuinely well-disciplined — almost everything is Mom-Test-clean, behavioral, and appropriately neutral, and two of its design choices (the WhatsApp-artifact probe in C, the "leave it for later" hook in I) are specifically well-suited to surfacing exactly the kind of guarded, unreported friction this persona is built to test. The wording is not the weak point. The weak point is structural and human: the highest-value data for a renter persona — the approval delay and the completely unreported small failure — sit in sections that are either late, optional, or first-on-the-chopping-block when the protected sections (especially F, which is exactly the kind of section that runs long with a real landlord story) eat into the clock, and the guide gives interviewers zero inline defense against the single most natural human reflex — a sympathetic aside right after hearing "he didn't reply for three days" — at the exact moment it's most likely to happen. Fix it with three small, specific edits before tomorrow: (1) add an inline no-editorializing reminder inside Section F right at the renter-permission block, (2) add the "how long did it actually take to get sorted/paid back" follow-up so the reimbursement-chase friction isn't merely optional, and (3) either protect the "leave it for later" sub-question from being the first cut or add a short, explicitly protected version of it earlier in the guide (Section C is the natural spot) — otherwise the guide is fine to send interviewers out with tomorrow.
