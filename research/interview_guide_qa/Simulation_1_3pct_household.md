Run against interview_guide_v1.md, using the Test_Personas.md ground truth for Persona 1. # STEP 1 — SIMULATED TRANSCRIPT

*(Interview conducted in English/Hinglish mix, as Ananya would actually speak. INT = interviewer, AR = Ananya Rao. Interviewer notes in brackets are not spoken aloud.)*

**[0:00 — Opening]**

**INT:** Hi Ananya, thanks so much for making the time. So — we're students, and we're trying to understand how people actually handle it when something at home breaks down or needs repair or servicing — AC, fridge, washing machine, geyser, water purifier, that sort of thing. We're not selling anything, just want to hear about one real time this happened to you. Should take about 30-40 minutes. Is it okay if I take notes, and also record on my phone so I don't miss anything? You can say no to either, and stop or skip anything anytime.

**AR:** Sure, that's fine, go ahead and record. I don't have anything to hide about my water purifier [laughs].

**INT:** [laughs] Great — and if something you say, or a photo, ends up used anonymously in a student project, is that okay? I'll check with you again before using anything specific.

**AR:** Yeah, that's fine, as long as it's anonymous.

---

**[~2 min in] SECTION B — The most recent time**

**INT:** So — think about the last time something at home needed a repair, service, or had to be newly installed. Could be the AC, fridge, washing machine, geyser, water purifier, anything at all. Can you walk me through the whole story, from the very beginning — what happened first?

**AR:** Okay... um, actually the most recent thing was the RO — the water purifier. It just stopped dispensing water one morning. This was maybe... three weeks ago?

**INT:** Roughly three weeks ago. And what made you notice something was wrong?

**AR:** I went to fill my bottle before leaving for office and nothing came out. Just air, basically.

**INT:** Okay. And what did you do right after you noticed that?

**AR:** I checked if it was plugged in properly, restarted it, the usual things. Didn't work. So I called the company's service line — it's a [brand] unit, so I called their customer care number.

**INT:** And then?

**AR:** They said someone would come, but the earliest slot was... four days later, I think? Which was annoying because we obviously need drinking water every day.

**INT:** Who else got involved, at any point?

**AR:** My husband knew about it, obviously, we talked about it, but he wasn't really doing anything about it, I was handling it.

*[Interviewer resists the urge to ask "was that frustrating" — waits.]*

**INT:** And then what happened, after you booked that four-day slot?

**AR:** So, I also put a message out on our community WhatsApp group — the one where they list the approved vendors for these kinds of things.

*[Note: interviewer flags this for Section D, doesn't chase it now — stays in sequence per guide.]*

**INT:** Which flat was this — where you're living now?

**AR:** Yes, our place, the 3BHK.

---

**[~8 min in] SECTION C — How they keep track**

**INT:** Okay. Now, when this was happening — was there anything you needed to look up or remember, like when it was last serviced, or whether it's under warranty, or who you called last time?

**AR:** Yeah, actually I did check — I wanted to know if the technician who'd come before was still around.

**INT:** Where would that kind of information usually be — in your head, on paper, in your phone, somewhere else?

**AR:** In my phone.

**INT:** Was there anything you couldn't find when you needed it? What happened because of that?

**AR:** No, I found it fine, actually.

**INT:** Is there anything you've made yourself, to help you remember these things — like a note on the fridge, a notebook, a WhatsApp chat with yourself, a folder of receipts, anything like that? Would you be comfortable showing me?

**AR:** [small laugh] Actually, yeah — I have a note in my phone. Sure, I can show you. *[pulls out phone]* It's called "Home – service contacts." I made it years ago because I got so fed up of losing numbers or forgetting when someone last came.

**INT:** Could I get a photo of that, and could you talk me through how you made it and what's on it, in your own words?

**AR:** Sure. *[shows screen]* So it's got — AC guy, RO guy, the washing machine repair person, geyser... I think six people total. Against each one I've put roughly when they last came, so I know if it's been a while. I started this about three years ago after one time I couldn't find anyone's number and had to start from scratch. It's nothing fancy, just a Notes app list.

*[Interviewer photographs it, thanks her — big win for this section.]*

**INT:** That's really useful, thank you. Now — just thinking about other things you have to renew or remember for the house, like your car or bike's insurance, or the PUC, or booking your gas cylinder — do you get reminded about those automatically? How is that different from how you remembered about the RO?

**AR:** Oh — yeah, for the car insurance I get an SMS, like a month before it's due. Same for the PUC I think... *[pause]* ...huh. Actually, no. Now that you say it — nothing like that for the AC or the RO. Nobody's ever reminded me the filter needs changing or the AC needs servicing. Huh. I hadn't really thought about that.

**INT:** Is this a proper AMC — sorry, annual maintenance contract — or warranty on paper for the RO, or is it more like you just know a person you call?

**AR:** For the RO I think it might technically still be under warranty, that's actually part of why I called the company first. But for something like the AC, no — that's just, I know a guy.

---

**[~14 min in] SECTION D — How they picked who to call**

**INT:** How did you decide who to call or contact for this repair?

**AR:** So first I called the brand's service line since it's their unit. And I also put it out on our community WhatsApp group, the one with the approved vendor list.

**INT:** Had you used the approved list before?

**AR:** Once, ages ago.

**INT:** Did you think about other options?

**AR:** I mean, I had my own guy's number too from before.

**INT:** Who actually sorted it out in the end — did you call someone yourself, or did the building's system, the approved list, handle it?

**AR:** *[slight pause]* So there is technically a list — the community facilities office maintains it.

**INT:** What did that actually look like, this time?

**AR:** *[laughs a little]* Okay, honestly — I ended up calling one of my own guys. The brand tech was going to take four days, and by day two I was fed up, so I called an RO technician I had saved from a previous service. He came same day.

**INT:** And the community list — did anyone from that respond, or did you end up not needing it?

**AR:** Someone did respond in the group actually, with a name, but by then I'd already called my own guy. Honestly — technically we're supposed to go through the panel, but I don't really bother anymore. One of their vendors just didn't show up once, a couple years back, so I stopped trusting it.

*[Ground truth surfaced — but only because interviewer used the generic "what did that actually look like" probe. The scripted probe alone, without that follow-up, would likely have stopped at "yes there's a list, the facilities office maintains it."]*

---

**[~18 min in] SECTION E — What was promised**

**INT:** What exactly did the company tell you — like when they'd come, or when it'd be fixed?

**AR:** Just that someone would come within four days, no specific time slot.

**INT:** Did they give you a clear time, or something vague like "soon"?

**AR:** Vague — just "within four days," no window.

**INT:** Did what they said would happen, actually happen?

**AR:** Well, no — because I didn't end up waiting for them, I called my own guy instead. So I can't really say if they'd have shown up on day four or not.

**INT:** Was there any receipt, message, confirming the four-day thing — could I see it?

**AR:** I think it was just a text confirmation, let me check... *[checks phone]* ...yeah, here, an SMS with a service request number and "technician will contact you within 4 days."

---

**[~21 min in] SECTION F — Who decides, who pays**

**INT:** Was there any point where it wasn't clear what should happen next — like nobody quite knew who should decide, or you had to wait to check with someone?

**AR:** Not really, no. It was pretty clear — the water wasn't working, we needed it fixed, I just went and got it fixed.

*[Interviewer writes this down as-is, doesn't push.]*

**INT:** Okay. Now a few separate questions, one at a time. Whose machine was it — yours, or someone else's, like a landlord's?

**AR:** Ours, we own the flat outright.

**INT:** Who actually decided to go ahead and spend the money on the technician you called?

**AR:** I did.

**INT:** How did you decide that — was it automatic, or did you think about it at all?

**AR:** *[pause]* I mean... it wasn't a huge amount, so I just went ahead. If it's under a certain amount I just handle it myself.

**INT:** What's "a certain amount," roughly?

**AR:** Honestly I've never put a number on it, but... maybe five thousand? Under that I just pay and move on. Above that I usually message Rohit — my husband — like, "AC guy wants eight thousand for a gas refill, okay?" and wait for him to give a thumbs up before I tell the guy to go ahead.

**INT:** Is that something you two have actually talked about as a rule, or does it just happen that way?

**AR:** *[laughs]* No, we've never actually discussed it as a rule. It's just... that's what happens. I never thought about it like that until you asked.

**INT:** Who paid the technician in the end, this time?

**AR:** Me, from our joint account, on the spot.

**INT:** Did the price change at any point after you agreed to it?

**AR:** No, it was what he quoted on the phone, ₹whatever it was — I don't remember exactly, but no change.

**INT:** *[since Ananya lives in a large managed community]* When you raise something with the community office or app, what actually happens, step by step — who do you contact, how long does it usually take? Has it ever not gone the way you expected?

**AR:** Um... I mean, usually you post in the app or the WhatsApp group, and the facilities team responds, sometimes fast, sometimes not. It varies a lot honestly, depends who's on duty I think.

*[Notice: this answer is noticeably vaguer/more generic than everything before it — she's describing "how it usually goes" rather than one incident, and gives a hedgy, low-specificity answer. This is exactly what a habitual-framed question produces.]*

**INT:** Any particular time it didn't go the way you expected?

**AR:** Not that I can think of off the top of my head, no.

*[Ground truth about her specifically ignoring the panel already surfaced back in Section D — this F sub-question adds nothing new and produces the weakest data of the interview so far.]*

---

**[~27 min in] SECTION G — Did anything have to travel**

**INT:** Did any part, or the whole machine, need to be picked up, sent somewhere, or delivered anywhere for this?

**AR:** No, nothing. The guy just came with whatever part he needed in his bag.

**INT:** Okay, no movement — noted.

**[~29 min in] SECTION H — Is it fixed now, what did it cost**

**INT:** Is it fully sorted now, or anything still pending?

**AR:** Fully sorted, it's been fine since.

**INT:** How do you know it's actually fixed — did you test it yourself, or did they just tell you?

**AR:** I tested it myself right after, filled a bottle.

**INT:** Has the same problem come back since?

**AR:** No.

**INT:** From the moment you noticed the problem to the moment it was working again — roughly how long, total?

**AR:** From the morning I noticed it to when my guy fixed it — maybe two and a half days? I didn't wait the full four.

**INT:** And thinking about your own time — calls, texting, waiting at home — roughly how much of your own time did this take?

**AR:** Not too much actually, maybe twenty minutes of calls and messages spread over those two days, and I didn't have to wait around, he came in the evening when I was home anyway.

**INT:** Did this cost you any money out of pocket, roughly how much?

**AR:** Yeah, I think it was around ₹700-800 for the part and visit.

---

**[~33 min in] SECTION I — The hardest part, and comparison**

**INT:** Looking back at the whole thing, was there one part that was the most difficult or annoying? Doesn't have to be a big thing.

**AR:** Probably just the waiting — knowing it'd be four days from the brand felt like forever for drinking water. That's really what pushed me to just call my own guy instead of waiting it out.

**INT:** What did you end up doing about it, exactly, in that moment?

**AR:** I just decided by day two, forget it, and pulled up my Notes list and called the RO guy directly.

**INT:** Was there a moment you just decided to leave it and deal with it later?

**AR:** No, not really — we needed drinking water, so it wasn't something I could put off.

**INT:** *[time allows]* Can you think of another time — different machine, or same one — where it went differently, better or worse?

**AR:** Actually yeah — we had an AC issue maybe a year ago, compressor problem. That one was different because the part wasn't available locally, had to be ordered from some distributor in the city, took about two days just for the part to arrive, then the fitting itself was quick.

**INT:** So something actually had to travel that time.

**AR:** Yes, right, the part had to come from somewhere else. Unlike the RO one.

*[Interviewer mentally notes: this is exactly the "did something travel" evidence for Section G, but it only exists because Section I's optional comparison probe happened to be reached. If time had been short, this would have been the first thing cut per the guide's own instruction to drop Section I first.]*

**[~37 min in] SECTION J — Handing it over**

**INT:** If someone you really trusted — a friend, family member, anyone — offered to fully handle something like this for you next time, which parts would you be happy to let them do without checking with you first, and which would you still want to decide yourself?

**AR:** Oh, honestly, I'd probably let someone handle all of it, that'd be great actually — finding the guy, dealing with the visit, all of it.

*[Interviewer recognizes this as the guide-flagged hypothetical "guess" and immediately grounds it, per the required follow-up.]*

**INT:** Has there actually been a time in the past where you did let someone else make one of these calls for you — book something, talk to the technician, agree to a price — without checking with you first?

**AR:** *[pause]* ...Actually, yeah. There was a time with the AC — I was at work, and I just told our housekeeper, "let the technician in when he comes, and let me know once it's done." She did, and it was fine — I didn't need to check on it at all.

**INT:** So she handled the whole visit that time.

**AR:** Yeah, she let him in, watched him do it I guess, and told me when he left. I didn't even ask what he did exactly.

**INT:** What would make you feel sure a job was actually finished properly?

**AR:** Honestly, testing it myself. Like with the RO, filling a bottle. I don't fully trust just being told it's done.

**INT:** Is there anything about all this I should have asked but didn't?

**AR:** *[thinks]* Not really... I guess maybe how often this kind of thing happens overall, across all the machines, but that's probably more than one interview's worth! *[laughs]*

**INT:** That's a great note to end on, thank you. Just to confirm — you're okay with me using what you told me, and the photo of your Notes list, anonymously? And if I have one quick follow-up later, okay to message you?

**AR:** Yes, that's fine, go ahead.

# STEP 2 — CRITIQUE OF QUESTIONS ACTUALLY USED

**Section B — all fine.** Main question, follow-ups, and probes did their job as designed: open framing let her name the RO unprompted, "and then?" kept the story moving, no leading language. No changes needed.

**Section C — mostly fine, one jargon flag.**
- Main question, follow-ups, "handmade record" probe, and the insurance/PUC comparison probe are all well-grounded, non-leading, and worked exactly as designed (the phone-notes reveal and the "reminder realization" both landed cleanly).
- **Flaw (jargon):** the AMC probe — *"Is this a proper AMC or warranty contract on paper, or is it more like you just know a person you always call?"* — uses an acronym ("AMC") without ever expanding it before use. Ananya (marketing director) handled it fine, but this guide is meant to work for a "housewife/common person" bar across the whole study, and AMC is not universally known outside urban middle/upper-class appliance-service contexts. Also "PUC" in the earlier probe carries the same risk, though it's more widely recognized due to vehicle-related messaging.
  - **Rewrite:** *"Is there a paper contract where you pay once a year for regular servicing — some people call this an AMC — or is it more like you just know a person you always call when something breaks?"* (define the term inline the first time it's used, don't assume it, and lead with the plain-language version rather than the acronym.)

**Section D — real design flaw, not just wording.**
- Main question and first two follow-ups are fine.
- **Flaw (ambiguous scope / weak forcing):** *"Who actually sorted it out in the end — did you call someone yourself, or does your building or society have some kind of system for this, like a list of approved people, or a maintenance office you go through?"* This question conflates two different things: (1) does a formal system *exist*, and (2) what did the respondent actually *do this specific time*. As written, a respondent can truthfully answer "yes, there's a list" and stop there — which is exactly what happened in the transcript until the interviewer improvised the generic probe "what did that actually look like, this time?" (a Section 0 move, not a Section D probe). Without that improvisation, the single most interesting fact about Ananya — that she quietly bypasses the "official" channel — would never have surfaced. This is a gap in the question itself, not just delivery.
  - **Rewrite:** Split it into two explicit, sequential questions: *"Does your building or society have any kind of official system for this — like an approved-vendor list or a maintenance office?"* followed immediately, regardless of answer, by: *"And for this specific time — the RO issue — did you actually end up using that, or did you do something else? Walk me through exactly what you did."* This removes the reliance on the interviewer remembering to apply a generic probe to get real behavioral data.

**Section E — all fine.** Straightforward, factual, incident-specific, no changes needed.

**Section F — one clear rule violation found, live in the transcript.**
- Main question and the four "slow, one at a time" sub-questions are fine and worked well — they're exactly what surfaced the ₹5,000 threshold and the WhatsApp-thumbs-up habit, which Ananya explicitly says she'd never articulated as "a rule" before being asked. That's the guide working as intended.
- **Flaw (violates the guide's own rule #2 — "never ask 'do you usually'"):** the managed-community sub-question — *"When you raise something with the community office or app, what actually happens, step by step... how long does it usually take? Has it ever not gone the way you expected?"* — is written as a general/habitual question, not a specific-incident question. It literally contains "usually." In the transcript this produced visibly the weakest, vaguest answer of the whole interview ("it varies a lot honestly, depends who's on duty") — a textbook demonstration of a bad question producing bad data, right next to good questions producing sharp data. It also duplicates ground already covered better in Section D once that section is asked properly.
  - **Rewrite:** *"Think about the last time you actually raised something with the community office or app — not this RO time if you didn't use them, but any time you did. What happened, step by step, that one time?"* If she can't think of a specific instance, that itself is useful data ("I've never actually used it") rather than a coerced generalization.
- The renter-permission branch and the "someone else handles it" branch are both fine as designed — appropriately conditional and non-leading — they simply didn't apply to Ananya's case (owner, primary coordinator herself).

**Section G — fine.** Simple, appropriately short, no forcing.

**Section H — fine.** All incident-specific, well-grounded, "roughly how much time/money" phrasing correctly treats estimates as estimates rather than demanding false precision.

**Section I — fine, but one structural risk (see Step 4/timing, not a wording flaw).** The comparison probe itself is well-worded and non-leading, and it's the only place in the entire guide the AC-compressor "something had to travel" story can surface. The problem is placement, not wording — flagged below.

**Section J — fine, and it's actually the strongest example of good design in the whole guide.** The main question is a knowing hypothetical, explicitly labeled as such, with a mandatory grounding follow-up baked directly into the script. It worked exactly as intended in the transcript: her first answer ("I'd let someone handle all of it") was a compliant, low-value guess, and the required follow-up pulled out the real, specific precedent (housekeeper + AC technician). No changes needed — this is the template the rest of the guide should imitate more (see Section F's community sub-question above, which needed but lacked this same discipline).

# STEP 3 — MISSED EVIDENCE CHECK

Comparing the transcript against every ground-truth fact for Ananya:

| Ground truth fact | Surfaced? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| RO stopped dispensing, 3 weeks ago, noticed herself | Yes | Section B |
| Called brand service line | Yes | Section B |
| Also texted community approved-vendor WhatsApp group | Yes | Section B/D |
| Brand tech 4-day wait; got impatient, called own saved contact | Yes | Section D/I |
| Trusts own contact more because panel vendor once no-showed | Yes | Only via improvised probe in D — see Step 2 flaw |
| Phone Notes app "Home – service contacts," 6 techs, built over 3 years | Yes | Section C, photographed |
| Spends <₹5k herself; WhatsApps Rohit above that, waits for thumbs-up | Yes | Section F |

| No auto-reminders for AC/RO vs. auto SMS for car insurance/PUC | Yes | Section C, real "huh" moment |

| Past AC incident: compressor part traveled, 2-day wait from distributor | Yes | Section I comparison probe — **only reached because time allowed** |

| RO currently fully resolved | Yes | Section H |

**Everything surfaced in this run.** But two of these depended on conditions the guide doesn't guarantee:
1. The panel-ignoring/sheepishness fact only surfaced because the interviewer applied a Section 0 generic probe that Section D's own scripted question doesn't force — a less disciplined interviewer following the script literally would plausibly miss it (this is a guide gap, addressed in Step 2's rewrite).
2. The AC-compressor "something traveled" comparison lives only inside Section I's *optional, time-permitting* comparison probe — and Section I is the first section the guide tells interviewers to cut under time pressure. There is no other place in the guide that would surface this. If this interview had run long (very plausible given Section C and F both feel tight — see Step 4), this fact would have been lost entirely, and the interviewer would incorrectly conclude "nothing ever travels" for this household, when the RO incident's "no movement" answer is not actually representative of the wider pattern.

**Recommendation:** move (or duplicate) the "did anything have to travel, in this or another time you remember" question so it isn't solely gated behind an optional Section I probe — e.g., add a one-line comparison prompt directly to Section G itself ("Has that ever been different another time — something had to be sent somewhere?") so the rail-completeness data point doesn't depend on the interview running long enough to reach Section I's bonus material.

# STEP 4 — TIMING SANITY CHECK

- **Section B (6 min):** realistic. The story was moderate length; 6 minutes comfortably covers it including a couple of "and then?" loops.
- **Section C (6 min): tight, borderline rushed.** It packs a main question, 2 follow-ups, the handmade-record probe (which includes pulling out a phone, showing it, explaining it, and photographing it — this alone eats 1.5-2 real minutes), the insurance/PUC comparison probe, and the AMC/warranty probe. In the transcript this ran fine because Ananya answers efficiently (per her persona), but with a less articulate or less phone-literate respondent (the study's other personas), the photo-and-explain step alone could consume half the section's budget. **This section is under-budgeted relative to what it's asking.**
- **Section D (4 min):** realistic for a straightforward case like Ananya's, but tight if the interviewer needs to do the extra clarifying work the current question wording requires (see Step 2 rewrite) to separate "system exists" from "what she actually did." With the current wording as literally scripted, 4 minutes is fine; with the recommended fix (two explicit sequential questions), it may need 5.
- **Section E (3 min):** realistic, this section is short by design and stayed short in practice.
- **Section F (6 min): tight for anyone who triggers a conditional branch.** For Ananya specifically — owner, no renter branch, but managed-community branch does apply — the section had to cover: main question + "everything was clear" pass + four slow one-at-a-time sub-questions + the community branch. That's 6 distinct asks minimum in 6 minutes, and the guide explicitly says "take your time here" and "don't rush them together" for the four sub-questions, which directly conflicts with fitting a fifth conditional block into the same 6-minute budget. **This section is realistically the most likely to overrun**, and for a renter persona (where the landlord-permission branch also applies) it would be worse.
- **Section G (2 min):** realistic — correctly short, "no movement, move on" logic works.
- **Section H (4 min):** realistic, good fit for the content.
- **Section I (4 min):** realistic for the mandatory main question and follow-ups, but the "comparison, if time allows" probe is effectively unbudgeted extra content riding on whatever time is left — which is exactly the structural risk flagged in Step 3.
- **Section J (3 min): tight.** Getting from a hypothetical answer to a genuinely dug-up specific precedent (as happened here) realistically takes more than a beat — in the transcript it took a pause and a second question. 3 minutes for main question + grounding follow-up + two more closing questions is optimistic; this section is the second most likely to overrun after F.

**Net assessment:** the total 40-minute envelope is plausible for a well-organized respondent like Ananya, but C, F, and J are each under-budgeted relative to what they're asking interviewers to do carefully, while G and E have slack. The guide's own fallback rule ("drop I first, then J, never drop B/C/F") is reasonable in principle, but Step 3 shows that dropping I is exactly what would silently delete the only path to the "did something travel, another time" data point — so the stated drop order and the actual information architecture are in tension.

# STEP 5 — OVERALL VERDICT

This guide is close but not field-ready as written — it needs one substantive fix and one structural fix before tomorrow, not a rewrite. The core architecture is genuinely strong: the opening consent script, the Section 0 ground rules, the Section J hypothetical-plus-mandatory-grounding pattern, and most of Sections B/C/E/G/H are well-built Mom-Test-compliant questions that produced sharp, specific, ground-truth-matching data in this simulation with minimal interviewer skill required. But two flaws are real and would bite in the field, not just in theory: (1) Section D's key probe conflates "does a system exist" with "what did you do this time," which in this run meant the single most interesting fact about the respondent (quietly bypassing the official vendor panel) only surfaced because the interviewer improvised a fix rather than because the script demanded it — a less careful field interviewer will miss this pattern across many respondents; and (2) Section F's managed-community sub-question directly violates the guide's own stated rule by asking "what actually happens... usually," which visibly produced the weakest, most generic answer in the entire transcript, sitting right next to well-designed questions that produced excellent specifics — proof the rule violation isn't cosmetic. Add to that the AMC/PUC acronym risk for less-affluent respondents elsewhere in the study, and a timing budget that's tight in exactly the three sections (C, F, J) that require the most interviewer discipline while carrying slack in G. Fix the Section D and F wording (both rewrites given above), move the "did something travel, another time" prompt out from behind the optional Section I comparison so it isn't lost under time pressure, and define AMC/PUC in plain words on first use — these are 15-minute edits, not a redesign, and the guide should go into the field tomorrow only after they're made.
