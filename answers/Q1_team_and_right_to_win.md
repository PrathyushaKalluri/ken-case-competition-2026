# Q1 — Who is this team, and why is this opening yours?

**Status: LOCKED except the two teammate lines.**
**Limits: one line per member · 50 words as a team.**

> **The question, verbatim:** Per member: your name and your closest personal connection to this pain. As a team: what you have that other teams don't — a person you can reach, a place you can observe, something you have already done about this problem. **If any three students at your college could submit your answer, rewrite it.**

---

## Part A — Per-member lines

### Sricharan — LOCKED (47 words)

> Our machines cost my middle-class family ₹10,000 a month, never evenly: my father finds the cash the month three things fail together, my mother loses hours to a chatbot she doesn't know is a chatbot, and I patch things up while we wait for someone real.

**The device: one bill, three currencies.** The father pays in money, the mother in time and confusion, Sricharan in labour. This lets one sentence carry three people's pain without turning into a list, and keeps it personal rather than analytical.

**Why each part is there:**
- *"middle-class"* — without it, ₹10,000 is just a number. With it, a judge knows why it hurts.
- *"never evenly"* — the real problem isn't the amount, it's the lumpiness. Money isn't available the month several things break at once. Nobody else will write this.
- *"the month three things fail together"* — makes "never evenly" concrete instead of abstract. This is the specificity the litmus test hunts for.
- *"a chatbot she doesn't know is a chatbot"* — the line a judge remembers. It names a failure that only exists because a machine is pretending to be a person, which is the terrain of this whole competition.
- *"someone real"* — quietly closes the loop back to the chatbot.

**Factual basis, in case it is ever queried:** ₹10,000/month is the father's real average spend on **machine repairs, servicing and replacement only** — confirmed as excluding society maintenance charges, domestic help and utilities. If that ever turns out to include society charges, change to *"keeping our home running costs my father ₹10,000 a month"*, which is true either way.

**Deliberately dropped:** the mutual-fund opportunity-cost framing. A personal-connection line is the wrong place for a returns calculation — a 2024 judge asked for the money question answered *"without using the words 'adjusted' and 'ebitda'"*. That argument is still available for Q9 or the business case.

## Part B — Team statement — LOCKED (exactly 50 words)

> Most teams will have one city and one side of this. We have places from all three tiers and three languages, and through family a carpenter running a ten-man crew who subcontracts the plumbers, electricians and technicians. We are the customer, and we know the people who do the work.

**What each part does:**

| Clause | Job it does |
|---|---|
| *"one city and one side of this"* | Names both gaps other teams have, in the opening sentence, so everything after reads as proof rather than description. Directly answers "what you have that other teams don't." |
| *"all three tiers and three languages"* | Tier structure, not place names — says these are structurally different markets, not just different addresses. Hyderabad (T1), Vizag (T2), Eluru and Hajipur, Bihar (T3). Two states, north and south. |
| *"a ten-man crew who subcontracts the plumbers, electricians and technicians"* | The hard, unclaimable fact. One relationship is a door to a whole trade network, not a single person. "One of our mothers has used the same carpenter for years" is claimable by anyone; a crew of ten is not. |
| *"We are the customer, and we know the people who do the work"* | Problem-market fit and go-to-market in one plain sentence — demand and supply, both already held. Deliberately jargon-free. No mention of agents, pilots, or anything that sounds pitched. |

**Why this framing and not the research.** Earlier drafts led with "we interviewed a carpenter" and "we photographed a ledger." That is Q2's evidence, not Q1's asset. Q1 asks what you **have**, present tense — a door you can still walk through. Judges are also choosing who to send into the rails round on 15 September, so what matters is standing access that doesn't run out, not work already finished.

**Word-count warning:** this is exactly at the 50 cap. If the live form counts differently (hyphens and "ten-man" are the usual culprits), drop "the" from *"the plumbers"* for one word of slack.
