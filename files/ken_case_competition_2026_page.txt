What you’re assembling
Ten answers: who you are, what you found out, how your agent works, and where it meets the rails.
Nothing here asks you to build or code. Four things go in: who your team is and why you have the right to win this opening; what you learned about the problem from the people who live it; how your agent would actually work, step by step; and where your solution meets the three rails, including which one you would bend.
Two of the ten ask what you believe rather than what you’d build. There is no right answer to either, and we’re looking for answers that are honest, authentic and could only have been written by you.
What comes after is when the competition really takes off. The strongest submissions from here move to the next stage, where ₹20 lakh in prize money is on the table and the rail partners open their doors — Pine Labs on payments, Delhivery on logistics, Gnani on voice, with sandboxes, documentation and time with their teams. Also, even if you don’t make it that far, the best answers from this round will be credited by team name in The Ken’s map of India’s agentic era, published in October and read by the founders, investors and operators building exactly this.
Your team · answer 01
01
Who is this team, and why is this opening yours?
One line per member · 50 words as a team
Per member: your name and your closest personal connection to this pain. As a team: what you have that other teams don’t — a person you can reach, a place you can observe, something you have already done about this problem. If any three students at your college could submit your answer, rewrite it.
The evidence · answer 02
We read this one first
02
The one customer insight you discovered.
60 words · proof optional, and scored · attach as many conversations as you have
One insight about this problem that is missing from our description of the openings, that you learned from people who live this pain. Talk to as many of them as you like — a parent, a tenant, a claimant, a cook, a landlord. The number of conversations is yours to decide; what we weigh is what they produced. Then choose the single sharpest thing you learned and write it in 60 words.
An insight is something non-obvious you now understand about the person you are building for — how they think about this problem, what they are really protecting, why they do something that looks irrational from the outside. It earns its place when it changes your solution: a step in your loop that exists because of it, or works differently because of it. Give us the insight, and show us where it landed in what you designed.
The sixteen openings exist because real people described their own lives to us, first-hand and in their own words. Your insight should arrive the same way. No AI tool can discover how human beings actually behave in the world, and that is precisely what we are looking for from you.
What you can attach (optional)
An audio or video recording of any conversation with one or more users, with a timestamp marking the moment the insight surfaced.
A photograph of the workaround itself , maybe the whiteboard on the fridge, the notebook, the folder of medical reports, the WhatsApp chat someone keeps with themselves. The systems people build by hand say more than what they tell you about them.
A survey you ran including the questions you asked and the raw answers, with a line on which answer you did not expect.
Your team’s working notes — whiteboard photos, hand-drawn flows, sticky notes from the session where this came together. Legibility is not a criterion.
Chat logs , including logs of you working the problem through with an AI
Your solution · answers 03 to 07
03
How your agent works, in six steps.
Six cells · one sentence each · 15 words max
What triggers it. What it already knows. What it does. Who it deals with, person or institution. What it asks its human, and when. How it knows it’s done. One sentence per step; if a step needs two, it is two steps.
04
How your solution touches each rail.
Payments · logistics · voice — one sentence each
State the role each rail plays. If a rail has no role in your opening, say so and give the reason.
05
Which rail will you innovate on?
Select one · 40 words
The rail where your solution needs something that does not exist today. Name the missing capability.
06
Which customer asset will you ask for?
30 words
The one thing the customer must hand over for your agent to work — order history, the family group, statements, contacts — and the reason they’ll agree.
07
The annexation: which use-case do you subsume next?
30 words
The use-case your solution takes over after this one, and why it falls to you rather than to whoever owns it today.
Your bets · answers 08 and 09
08
Which of the sixteen openings would you never hand to an assistant?
One opening · one sentence
Assume the assistant works flawlessly. Name the opening and the reason. There is no right answer.
09
Which Indian company should have built this already?
One company · 60 words
Pick a company that has the users, the data and the payment relationship your solution needs. Make your best guess why they haven’t built this yet.
Your track · answer 10
10
Which track are you entering?
Select one · locks with your submission
Your team enters one track together. You gave a preference when you registered; this is where it becomes final. Product Strategy teams finish the competition with a fully specified build — complete flows, the segment map, the wall you hit and why. Product Build teams finish with a working prototype on a partner sandbox. Both have separate prizes and are judged by the quality of your evidence, the depth of your load-bearing rail, the specificity of your ask.
The ground rules
No code is required to advance, reach the finale, or win.
One opening per team. You can change your mind until you submit; your evidence starts over when you do.
Every member of a team must be from the same institution, and one email belongs to one team.
Get consent from everyone you interview, and tell them their words may be published. Parts of what you submit — a quote, a photograph, a line from a recording — may appear in The Ken’s map of India’s agentic era, credited to your team.
Quotes are published the way our openings document does it: age band and city, never a name.
The Ken may validate anything submitted here, and may disqualify a team at its sole discretion.

The Case
Sometime soon, Indians will stop doing things they’ve done for decades. They’ll stop comparing the same product across three e-commerce apps before they buy it. They’ll stop sharing their location with every delivery partner on WhatsApp. They’ll stop setting a 9:58am alarm to fight for a tatkal ticket.
An AI agent will do all of it, and more.
For twenty years, Indian businesses have been built on owning your attention, your habit, or your indecision, and an agent has none of the three. As agents take over, value pools somewhere new and drains from someone old. This competition exists to work out where it goes, in what order, and what strange new companies get built in the space that opens up on the other side of The Great Rewiring.
And you — students on campuses across the country — will be the ones who give the rest of us the first glimpse of this future. This happens across three stages:
Stage 1: Choose
First, you’ll have to pick what you want to solve.
You’ll get actual problems handed over by actual humans. Hundreds of The Ken’s readers are telling us all the annoying, frustrating and repetitive parts of their daily lives, and the quirky workarounds they’ve built to handle them. They’re sharing vivid stories of how they currently struggle to solve problems across categories like health and fitness, money, transport, household, parenting, pets and more. We’ll cluster those into dozens of opportunity spaces: each one a job someone wants done, with the evidence behind it, and no solution yet.
Stage 2: Assembly
Then you put the pieces together to design your agent.
This is where your product strategy chops are tested. You’ll have to discover a unique consumer insight around which you assemble your agent. As with most consumer products, this insight is often unexpected and surprising — sometimes it’s about asking users to hand over something they own, like their payment history, or access to their WhatsApp groups. In return, you’ll have to make the case for how your agent will fundamentally rewire their life and solve a deep, emotional problem they experience every day.
Finally, you’ll have to design your agent across the three rails any agent has to be built on if it is going to work in the real world:
Voice — how it talks to people.
Payments and authorisation — what it is allowed to do, and how it pays and gets paid.
Logistics — how it moves things in the physical world.
Stage 3: Build
This is when you test your solution against the real world.
You’ll take your solution to the companies that are at the frontier of building agentic infrastructure in India: Gnani for voice, Pine Labs for payments and authorisation, and Delhivery for logistics. In this round, you’ll interact with their teams, get access to their rails and infrastructure, and build agentic prototypes. Teams on the build track will go deeper and create agents, while those on the product strategy track will present their solutions and test them against what each rail can actually do today.
This round is all about testing the limits of India’s agentic infrastructure, where it bends and breaks, and making recommendations about what needs to be changed and why.
The top teams will fight it out at a finale, in front of a jury of India’s leading founders, CEOs, academics, operators, and market experts, for ₹20 lakh in cash prizes. There are prizes for the most innovative solutions across each of the rails.
Finally, the best and most innovative solutions are then synthesised into The Ken’s map of how agentic AI rewires India, published in October and read across the industry. This will be the report that companies, startups, and VCs read and reread for years to understand how the disruption plays out: what gets built, where it comes from, where consumers find new value, who wins, and who’s exposed.
The student teams whose solutions get listed here will be the ones companies compete to recruit and hire — or who build the next wave of influential agentic startups and products.
The Partners
Title partner
Zerodha is India’s second-largest stockbroker and a fintech pioneer. It serves millions of retail investors, offering zero-brokerage equity trading, derivatives and commodities trading, and more. It was also one of the first stockbrokers in the country to integrate MCP and open up their systems to agents.
Logistics partner
Delhivery is India’s largest logistics network, and has just opened test access to its Maps and MCP systems to outside developers for the first time. As logistics partner, they’ll work with and support teams whose agents have to make something physically arrive.
Payments and authorisation partner
Pine Labs, India's leading payments company, has already shipped agentic payments end to end. The Pine Labs Payments Protocol (P3P) is the protocol that allows agent-to-agent autonomous payments over UPI and cards on someone's behalf; Grantex sets what that agent may spend, on whose authority, and keeps the record of what it did. As a payments and authorisation partner, they'll open both to teams whose agents have to move real money.
Voice partner
Gnani is India’s frontier Voice AI company — building real-world speech across every accent, language, and condition that production deployments demand. As voice partner, they’ll open up their APIs for teams who build innovative solutions that are voice-first.
Eligibility
The Great Rewiring is open to full-time college students, UG or PG, at any institution in India.
A team is one, two or three students. Most of the work rewards three.
Everyone on a team must be from the same institution.
There is no cap on how many teams one institution can enter.
For both tracks, product strategy or building, you pick your track when you register and can change your mind up to the build stage.
No code is required to advance, reach the finale, or win.
The Ken may validate anything a team submits, and may disqualify a team at its sole discretion.
Judging
Every submission is read against the same five things, whichever track you enter and whether or not you write code.
Evidence. Did a real person tell you this? Interviews, recordings, and what you found that you weren’t looking for while designing the solution.
Creativity. Is the solution fresh, counterintuitive, unique, and different? This is usually where AI-generated solutions fail.
Clarity. Is the solution communicated clearly? Is it specific?
Feasibility. Is it a practical solution? Can it be implemented in a reasonable way?
Thoroughness. Is the solution meticulous and detailed?

For the readers
What would you rewire first?
Every Indian life runs on three lists: the endlessly repeated, the dreaded-but-unavoidable and the forever-postponed. All of these are held together by strange workarounds, like a WhatsApp group with one member in it, an app that’s half-logged, or a human agent whose only job is to send you gentle reminders.
Tell us about yours.
As you go, you’ll see what other subscribers confessed: some of it quirky, some eerily familiar.
This is just the beginning. What you name here becomes the problem statement — over the next few weeks, India’s sharpest student teams will compete to build AI assistants for annoyances like the ones you face, and you’ll get first go at testing what they make and telling them where it falls short.
And it all ends in a published map, which is The Ken’s account of how India rewires itself. You’ll be the first to read it.
